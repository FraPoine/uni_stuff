## Ethics values

• **Autonomy**: The right of people to make free and knowledgable decisions, without being manipulated or limited by external systems.

• **Dignity**: The intrinsic and invaluable value of every human being, which must always be respected and never reduced to a simple "data" or means to be exploited.

• **Privacy**: The right of individuals to maintain control over their personal information and to be protected from unauthorised data collection or surveillance.

• **Fairness**: The commitment to act without prejudice (bias) and without discrimination, ensuring that decisions and systems treat everyone in an objective and balanced way.

• **Equity / Solidarity**: The promotion of equal opportunities for all and mutual support within the community, with the aim of not leaving the most vulnerable groups behind.

• **Social Wellbeing**: The positive and constructive impact that an action or technology should have on society, helping to improve the quality of collective life.

• **Explainability**: The ability to understand and explain clearly and transparently how a system (such as an algorithm or an AI) has come to make a certain decision.

• **Safety**: The guarantee that a system is reliable, robust and designed so as not to cause physical, psychological or material damage to humans or the environment.

• **Performance**: The effectiveness, efficiency and accuracy with which a system fulfils the task for which it was designed, ensuring that it works as promised.

# Social Wellbeing and Conclusion

The last ethical value we decided to explore is **Social Wellbeing**.  
Social Wellbeing is based on a consequentialist idea: when we evaluate a decision, we should look at its overall consequences for all the people affected. In simple terms, a technology is ethically better if it increases the wellbeing of the community as a whole and reduces overall harm.  
In this case, the affected community includes people with eating disorders, their families, volunteers, healthcare workers, and the organization providing the service.

In this case, NEDA suspended the chatbot after it gave harmful advice, such as calorie counting and weight loss suggestions, to vulnerable users with eating disorders.  
This raises a key Social Wellbeing question: can a benefit for many people justify a serious risk for a few?

From another point of view, the chatbot could increase social wellbeing, depending on who we look at.

First, imagine a young person with an eating disorder. Maybe they feel ashamed, scared, or not ready to speak with a real person. For them, a chatbot that is always available could feel like an easier first step. This is not unrealistic: KFF found that many people already use AI for mental health advice, especially young adults between 18 and 29. This is important because eating disorders often affect young people.

Now imagine a worker or volunteer. They have too many requests to answer, and many of them are emotionally difficult. In NEDA’s case, a small team and many volunteers had to deal with almost 70,000 contacts in one year. From this point of view, AI could help reduce waiting times and give more people at least a first answer.

But then imagine a parent. Their child uses the chatbot and starts getting worse after receiving advice about calories or weight loss. The parent may not even understand what is happening at first. From this point of view, the chatbot does not look helpful anymore. It looks like a hidden danger.

So the ethical problem is not simple. The same chatbot can look helpful, necessary, or dangerous depending on who is affected. Social Wellbeing asks us to look at all these people together, and to ask: who benefits, who is harmed, and how do we balance these consequences?

## conclusion

The Tessa case does not give us a simple solution. Instead, it raises an ethical question: when we use AI in mental health, are we improving access to care, or are we replacing human support with a cheaper and less trustworthy system?

# Social Wellbeing and Conclusion

The last ethical value we decided to explore is **Social Wellbeing**.

Social wellbeing means looking at the impact of a technology not only on a single user, but on the whole community affected by it. In this case, the community includes people with eating disorders, their families, volunteers, healthcare workers, and organizations like NEDA.

The main ethical question is not simply: **“Was the chatbot good or bad?”**  
The deeper question is: **“Does replacing or reducing human support with an AI chatbot improve the wellbeing of this community, or does it create new risks?”**

From one point of view, the chatbot could be seen as a way to increase access. It can be available 24/7, it can answer many people at the same time, and it can reduce waiting times. This could be especially important for young people, who are already used to using chatbots and online tools to ask for help.

From the point of view of an organization, the chatbot could also seem like a practical response to a real problem: too many requests, limited resources, and human volunteers under pressure.

However, from the point of view of a vulnerable user, the situation looks very different. A person with an eating disorder may not only need information. They may need empathy, reassurance, and someone able to understand the seriousness of their condition. If the chatbot gives harmful advice, such as calorie counting or weight loss suggestions, the damage is not only individual. It can reduce trust in the whole support system.

From the point of view of volunteers and workers, there is another issue. Replacing human support with a cheaper automated system can make people feel that their emotional labour and experience are not valued. It also raises the question of whether technology is being used to improve care or simply to cut costs.

So, the value of social wellbeing depends a lot on the perspective we choose. For an organization, the chatbot may represent efficiency and scalability. For users, it may represent risk and lack of human care. For workers, it may represent replacement. For families, it may represent fear that their children or loved ones are receiving unsafe support.

For this reason, the Tessa case is important not because it gives us an easy answer, but because it shows a difficult ethical tension: **how can we balance access, efficiency, safety, human dignity, and trust in public health services?**

To conclude, the main issue is not only whether this specific chatbot failed. The broader issue is deciding what kind of support we consider acceptable for vulnerable people. If AI is introduced in mental health, we must ask who benefits, who takes the risk, and whose wellbeing is being prioritized.
